The 50/30/20 rule, explained

Guide 3 min read Updated 2 November 2026
In short

The 50/30/20 rule splits your take-home pay three ways: 50% for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for savings and extra debt repayments. It is a simple starting point, not a law. If your rent is high, bend it to fit.

Where does the rule come from?

The split was popularised by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in their 2005 book All Your Worth. Its appeal is that you only track three numbers instead of dozens of categories.

What goes in each bucket?

  • Needs (50%): rent or mortgage, groceries, power, transport, insurance, phone and minimum debt repayments.
  • Wants (30%): eating out, streaming, clothes, hobbies, holidays and anything else you could pause.
  • Savings (20%): an emergency fund, savings goals and any repayments above the minimum.

The rule uses take-home pay, after tax. Your employer's super contributions are paid on top of your wage, so they are not part of the 20%.

Not sure which bucket something belongs in? Our needs vs wants guide covers the grey areas.

What it looks like on an Australian pay

Here is the split for an example take-home pay of $1,200 a week, shown per week, fortnight and month:

BucketPer weekPer fortnightPer month
Take-home pay$1,200$2,400$5,200
Needs (50%)$600$1,200$2,600
Wants (30%)$360$720$1,560
Savings (20%)$240$480$1,040

Example figures only. A month here is 52 weeks divided by 12, which is why it is more than four weeks of pay.

When should you bend it?

The rule was never meant to fit everyone. Some common reasons to adjust it:

  • High rent. In a big city, needs can easily pass 50%. A 60/20/20 or 70/20/10 split is still a plan.
  • Paying off debt. If you are clearing a credit card or personal loan, you might move some of your wants into the 20% for a while.
  • Saving for something big. A house deposit or a wedding might mean a bigger savings slice for a set time.
  • Irregular income. If your pay changes week to week, base the split on a lower, typical pay and save anything above it.

What matters is that the numbers add up to your pay and that you pick them on purpose.

How to try it this week

Write down your take-home pay. Add up last month's needs from your bank statement and work out what percentage of your pay they took. Do the same for savings. Whatever is left went on wants. Compare your real split with 50/30/20, then pick one small change to move toward the split you want.

Key takeaways

The 50/30/20 rule in five points

  • Split take-home pay into needs, wants and savings.
  • Minimum repayments are needs. Extra repayments are savings.
  • Employer super is separate and does not count toward the 20%.
  • High rent or debt is a reason to bend the split, not abandon it.
  • Start with your real split, then move one step at a time.
